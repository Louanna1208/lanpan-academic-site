Research interests

Advances in language models and representation learning make it increasingly practical to connect information across large collections of papers and behavioral datasets. My work brings together two foundations: reusable behavioral evidence and methods for comparing claims, tasks, and cognitive processes. The four interests below connect these foundations to questions about human cognition. They are complementary ways to develop and test explanations, rather than stages that every project must follow.

Aligning scientific representations

When do differently described studies test the same claim, and what experiment would distinguish competing explanations?

A claim can be stated in a paper, tested through a task, and explained by a computational model. But shared terminology does not guarantee that two studies test the same claim, and different terminology can obscure a genuine connection. I am interested in making the relationships among claims, manipulations, measurements, and models explicit, so we can assess when findings provide evidence about the same mechanism.

Comparing studies should help us see what the evidence already tells us and what still needs to be tested. For example, two theories may explain the same existing results but predict different outcomes when an experimental condition changes. An experiment under that condition could help distinguish the theories. I want to connect hypotheses with the experiments that test them so we can identify these unresolved questions and decide which experiment would help answer them.

Measuring relationships between tasks

Can a task's structure predict its transfer pattern, and can that prediction guide the design of a new experiment?

Do task labels and descriptions capture the processes expressed in human behavior? Tasks that sound similar may recruit different processes, while differently named tasks may share computational structure. I am interested in measuring these relationships through behavior and comparing them with expert classifications and semantic similarity.

Task Space measures relationships through behavioral transfer; experimental representations make task structure explicit. I want to test whether these two descriptions can predict one another, and use their disagreements to identify informative experiments.

A next question is whether a new task's experimental structure can predict its transfer relationships before its behavioral data are collected. Suppose transfer appears to depend on action-outcome contingencies rather than a task's cover story. We could then predict how transfer should change when contingencies change but the story stays fixed, and compare this with changing the story while preserving the contingencies. Such experiments would test what accounts for the measured task relationships. Connecting this model-based measure to human cognitive mechanisms requires independent behavioral tests.

Building cumulative behavioral evidence

When does a behavioral effect support the same explanation across experiments, and what can its boundaries tell us to test next?

Comparing experimental contexts lets us ask more than whether an effect holds on average. Where does it appear, disappear, or reverse? Does the same behavioral pattern have the same meaning across settings? My interest is in using these comparisons to explain why an effect changes and predict what will happen under conditions that have not yet been tested.

More broadly, I want to use cross-study patterns to locate conditions where competing explanations predict different outcomes, then test those predictions in new experiments. The scientific value of a boundary is the opportunity to explain and predict what happens on either side of it.

Understanding real-world decisions

When laboratory theories fail to predict real-world choices, which processes are missing, and can incorporating them improve predictions in new situations?

A difference between laboratory preferences and real-world, high-stakes choices need not mean that a person's risk preference has changed. The environment may instead change their goals, what they compare an outcome against, or how they understand the problem. Can language and behavioral trajectories provide independent measures of these goals and comparison standards, rather than inferring them only from choices? Such measurements could help distinguish explanations that make similar predictions in the laboratory but diverge in a new context.

The research path is to identify situations where laboratory predictions fail, propose a missing process, and manipulate that process under controlled conditions. The crucial test is then whether a model incorporating it predicts previously unobserved real-world choices. This makes real-world behavior a source of better laboratory experiments and revised cognitive explanations, as well as a test of their validity.

Longer term, I am interested in how AI systems form, express, compress, and revise cognitive rules. Connecting task structure, behavior, and internal representations could help test when a stated rule actually governs a system's decisions, and what makes that rule change.
