Lan Pan

Computational cognitive science

I am interested in how cognitive mechanisms carry across tasks and situations, and how we can measure them more directly. My research uses behavioral data, language, and computational models to connect findings across studies and test the reach of cognitive theories.

I work with Robert C. Wilson and Hanbo Xie at Georgia Tech, Sebastian Musslick at Osnabrück University, and Akshay K. Jagadish and Younes Strittmatter at Princeton University.

Research interests

My broader aim is a computable, cumulative cognitive science: one in which we can compare what tasks measure, use multiple sources of evidence to identify mechanisms, and build on findings across studies. Three questions guide my work.

Cognitive mechanisms across tasks

When do different tasks rely on the same cognitive process? Similar task names or behavioral outcomes provide only part of the answer. I am interested in using learning and transfer, together with models' internal representations, to identify shared and task-specific mechanisms.

Our work on exploration connects strategies in language models to representations of uncertainty and empowerment at different model layers. This provides a starting point for studying how behaviorally relevant computations are represented within models.

Pan, L.*, Xie, H.*, & Wilson, R. C. (2025). Large language models think too fast to explore effectively. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, 38, 102480-102508. [DOI] [arXiv]

* Equal contribution.

Ongoing. Cross-task transfer among models trained on individual psychology tasks; event-state representations for comparing experimental paradigms, with cognitive control as an initial domain.

Language and decision processes

How do people understand a decision problem, and how does that understanding shape their choices? Different cognitive models can explain the same choices. Verbal reports offer additional evidence about the beliefs, strategies, and reference points that generate behavior.

In our work on automated cognitive model discovery, adding think-aloud data improved prediction of held-out decisions and changed the structures of the models that were discovered. This motivates my interest in language as a source of constraints on explanations of decision-making.

Xie, H., Jagadish, A. K., Pan, L., & Wilson, R. C. (2026). Think-aloud reshapes automated cognitive model discovery beyond behavior [Conference presentation]. 9th Annual Conference on Cognitive Computational Neuroscience (CCN). [arXiv]

Ongoing. Using language and behavioral trajectories from Deal or No Deal to study how people represent high-stakes decisions, and whether mechanisms inferred from laboratory measures also explain real-world choices.

Evidence across studies

How can findings accumulate when studies use different terms, tasks, and datasets? I am interested in methods that make these differences explicit so researchers can find relevant evidence and determine what can be compared or combined.

Our hypothesis-literature matching work compares claims and papers at the level of theoretical constructs. It examines whether a hypothesis has already been tested even when the literature uses different wording, and evaluates the evidence for a match.

Pan, L., & Musslick, S. (2026). Has someone already tested this? Construct-level hypothesis-literature matching. Discovery Science 2026 (accepted).

Ongoing. Within the PSYCHE collaboration, my work focuses on an agent and analysis tools built on a behavioral database developed by collaborators. The goal is to help researchers find and analyze relevant data across thousands of datasets, asking questions that a single study cannot answer.
