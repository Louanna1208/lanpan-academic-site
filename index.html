Computational cognitive science

I ask when findings from different tasks and settings can be treated as evidence about the same cognitive mechanism. My research uses language, behavioral data, and computational models to measure these relationships and investigate where cognitive theories apply.

I work with Robert C. Wilson and Hanbo Xie at Georgia Tech, Sebastian Musslick at Osnabrück University, and Akshay K. Jagadish and Younes Strittmatter at Princeton University.

Research interests

My aim is a computable, cumulative cognitive science. A construct may be described in a paper, operationalized in a task, observed in behavior, and explained by a model. Establishing whether these representations concern the same mechanism connects four research interests: comparability, transfer, the boundaries of effects, and real-world validity.

Aligning scientific representations

What makes findings from different studies comparable? Hypotheses, experimental manipulations, behavioral effects, and computational models express different aspects of a scientific claim. I am interested in making their relationships explicit so we can assess whether they provide evidence about the same mechanism.

The starting point is a scientific question: which studies use a similar manipulation but report different effects, or which intervention would distinguish competing models? These questions determine what a computational representation needs to preserve and how to test its usefulness.

Our hypothesis-literature matching work compares claims and papers through their theoretical constructs. It provides a first step toward comparing evidence across differences in terminology.

Pan, L., & Musslick, S. (2026). Has someone already tested this? Construct-level hypothesis-literature matching. Discovery Science 2026 (accepted).

Ongoing. Extending this question to experimental factors and task structure through event-state representations, with cognitive control as an initial domain.

Measuring relationships between tasks

Do task labels and descriptions capture the processes expressed in human behavior? Tasks that sound similar may recruit different processes, while differently named tasks may share computational structure. I am interested in measuring these relationships through behavior and comparing them with expert classifications and semantic similarity.

Ongoing. Using LLM fine-tuning as a measurement tool: within a common model backbone, an adapter is trained on human behavior from one task and evaluated on whether it improves prediction of human behavior in another. Transfer patterns define an operational measure of behavioral-computational relationships; further tests examine which behavioral features account for them.

Our exploration study provides a methodological foundation: analyses of model representations connect exploratory strategies to uncertainty and empowerment. This motivates using language models and their internal representations to investigate cognitive processes across tasks.

Pan, L.*, Xie, H.*, & Wilson, R. C. (2025). Large language models think too fast to explore effectively. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, 38, 102480-102508. [DOI] [arXiv]

Mapping the boundaries of behavioral effects

Under what conditions does a behavioral effect appear, disappear, or reverse? Comparing datasets across experimental conditions can reveal boundaries that remain invisible within a single study. I am interested in describing an effect as a function of task features, timing, learning history, and other design choices.

The scientific goal is to determine which theoretical accounts explain which regions of this experimental space, and whether those accounts predict the outcomes of new experiments.

Ongoing · PSYCHE. Working with Akshay K. Jagadish and Younes Strittmatter on an agent and analysis tools built on a behavioral database developed by collaborators. My contribution focuses on helping researchers find and analyze relevant evidence across thousands of datasets, with the aim of tracing comparable behavioral patterns across experiments.

Understanding real-world decisions

Do mechanisms inferred from laboratory measures explain real-world, high-stakes choices? A task environment can change how a person understands a problem, which may in turn change the process that generates a decision. I am interested in measuring these subjective representations through language and behavioral trajectories.

Our work on automated cognitive model discovery shows that think-aloud data can improve prediction of held-out decisions and change the structures of the models discovered. This supports using language to constrain explanations that behavior alone may leave unresolved.

Xie, H., Jagadish, A. K., Pan, L., & Wilson, R. C. (2026). Think-aloud reshapes automated cognitive model discovery beyond behavior [Conference presentation]. 9th Annual Conference on Cognitive Computational Neuroscience (CCN). [arXiv]

Ongoing. Studying consequential choices in settings where options, outcomes, and timing can be reconstructed, to test how subjective problem representations and decision mechanisms change beyond the laboratory.
