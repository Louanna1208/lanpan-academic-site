Lan Pan

Computational cognitive science

I ask when findings from different tasks and settings can be treated as evidence about the same cognitive mechanism. Using language, behavioral data, and computational models, I study how tasks and theoretical claims relate to the processes expressed in behavior. Clearer representations of these relationships should help us make better predictions about human behavior we have not yet observed, and decide which experiments could test, overturn, or improve our explanations.

I work with Robert C. Wilson and Hanbo Xie at Georgia Tech, Sebastian Musslick at Osnabrück University, and Akshay K. Jagadish and Younes Strittmatter at Princeton University.

Research interests

My aim is a computable, cumulative cognitive science. A construct may be described in a paper, operationalized in a task, observed in behavior, and explained by a model. Connecting these representations should allow existing findings to constrain what we expect from an experiment that has not yet been run. The four interests below form a research cycle: make evidence comparable, learn which task features matter, predict where explanations will succeed or fail, and use new experiments to revise both the representations and the theories.

Aligning scientific representations

Once studies become comparable, which untested predictions and decisive experiments become identifiable?

Hypotheses, experimental manipulations, behavioral effects, and computational models express different aspects of a scientific claim. I am interested in making their relationships explicit so we can assess whether they provide evidence about the same mechanism.

The goal is to identify where a theory's claims extend beyond the conditions in which it has been tested. If a mechanism is proposed to generalize across sensory modalities but the evidence is entirely visual, that gap suggests a specific test. Representing claims alongside experimental factors could help distinguish old hypotheses in new wording from untested predictions. The next step is to formulate those predictions and select experiments where competing explanations disagree. This gives a gap scientific value: testing it could change which explanation the evidence supports, rather than simply add another study to the literature.

Our hypothesis-literature matching work asks whether a proposed hypothesis has already been tested, even when the relevant studies use different terminology. We represent both the query and claims extracted from paper abstracts in terms of their predictor and outcome constructs, the relationship between them, and any stated conditions. This makes it possible to distinguish papers that discuss related concepts from those that support or contradict the specific claim.

In an evaluation using 107 expert-extracted hypothesis-paper pairs and 1,605 synthetic unsupported hypotheses, construct-level matching correctly rejected 87–90% of the unsupported hypotheses, compared with 31–40% when asking an LLM directly. The contribution is a testable way to compare claims beyond wording, while separating conceptual overlap from evidence for a particular relationship. It provides a first step toward comparing evidence across differences in terminology.

Ongoing. Extending this question to experimental factors and task structure through event-state representations, with cognitive control as an initial domain.

Measuring relationships between tasks

Can a task's structure predict its transfer pattern, and can that prediction guide the design of a new experiment?

Do task labels and descriptions capture the processes expressed in human behavior? Tasks that sound similar may recruit different processes, while differently named tasks may share computational structure. I am interested in measuring these relationships through behavior and comparing them with expert classifications and semantic similarity.

Ongoing. Using LLM fine-tuning as a measurement tool: within a common model backbone, an adapter is trained on human behavior from one task and evaluated on whether it improves prediction of human behavior in another. Transfer patterns define an operational measure of behavioral-computational relationships; further tests examine which behavioral features account for them.

A next question is whether a new task's experimental structure can predict its transfer relationships before its behavioral data are collected. Suppose transfer appears to depend on action-outcome contingencies rather than a task's cover story. We could then predict how transfer should change when contingencies change but the story stays fixed, and compare this with changing the story while preserving the contingencies. Such experiments would test what accounts for the measured task relationships. Connecting this model-based measure to human cognitive mechanisms requires independent behavioral tests.

Our exploration study provides a methodological foundation for this approach. We compared language models with 29,493 human players in an open-ended discovery game, examining two strategies: seeking uncertain outcomes and choosing actions that expand future possibilities, known as empowerment. Humans used both, whereas several non-reasoning models relied more heavily on uncertainty and made less use of empowerment.

Sparse autoencoders identified internal features associated with these theory-defined quantities. In LLaMA-3.1-70B, uncertainty and choice-related signals were strongest in early layers, while empowerment was represented most strongly later in the network. Ablating an empowerment-related feature selectively reduced empowerment-guided behavior, linking the representation to the model's strategy through intervention. Together, these analyses connect behavioral patterns, cognitive theory, and internal computation. They motivate using language models as measurement tools for studying cognitive processes, with representations that can be examined and experimentally perturbed.

Mapping the boundaries of behavioral effects

Why do effect boundaries occur, and can we predict what happens when we cross them?

Under what conditions does a behavioral effect appear, disappear, or reverse? Comparing datasets across experimental conditions can reveal boundaries that remain invisible within a single study. I am interested in describing an effect as a function of task features, timing, learning history, and other design choices.

Consider two theories that both explain a positive effect under familiar conditions. With less preparation time, one might predict that the effect disappears, while the other predicts that it reverses. Cross-study analysis could help locate this region of disagreement; a targeted experiment could then distinguish the explanations. The boundary becomes a source of new theoretical tests, rather than just a description of where an effect stops.

My goal is to use evidence across experiments to develop predictions about how effects depend on experimental conditions, and identify new experiments that expose weaknesses in current explanations. Those predictions need to be tested with newly collected data, separating the proposed process from other differences between the original studies.

Ongoing · PSYCHE. Working with Akshay K. Jagadish and Younes Strittmatter on an agent and analysis tools built on a behavioral database developed by collaborators. My contribution focuses on helping researchers find and analyze relevant evidence across thousands of datasets. These tools are being developed to support the cross-experiment analyses needed to formulate and test such condition-specific predictions.

Understanding real-world decisions

When laboratory theories fail to predict real-world choices, which processes are missing, and can incorporating them improve predictions in new situations?

A difference between laboratory preferences and real-world, high-stakes choices need not mean that a person's risk preference has changed. The environment may instead change their goals, what they compare an outcome against, or how they understand the problem. Language and behavioral trajectories can provide additional constraints on these explanations, helping specify candidate processes and ways to test them.

The research path is to identify situations where laboratory predictions fail, propose a missing process, and manipulate that process under controlled conditions. The crucial test is then whether a model incorporating it predicts previously unobserved real-world choices. This makes real-world behavior a source of better laboratory experiments and revised cognitive explanations, as well as a test of their validity.

Our work on automated cognitive model discovery asks whether people's verbal accounts can help distinguish explanations that their choices alone leave unresolved. Using risky decisions from 72 participants, we compared LLM-generated cognitive models discovered from choices alone with models discovered from choices and think-aloud reports together. Adding language improved model fit on held-out decisions at the group level.

Crucially, language changed the computational structures of the discovered models, not just their fit: 69.4% of participants' best-fitting models moved to a different structural family. A common shift was from explicitly comparing option values to first transforming and integrating information within each option. These results show that verbal reports can constrain which explanations model discovery selects, beyond what behavior alone provides. This offers a basis for investigating how subjective interpretations shape decisions, without assuming that a better fit by itself establishes the underlying human mechanism.

Ongoing. Studying consequential choices in settings where options, outcomes, and timing can be reconstructed, to test how subjective problem representations and decision mechanisms change beyond the laboratory.
