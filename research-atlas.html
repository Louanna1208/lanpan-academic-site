Research Atlas

A place to return to your research ideas.

Try the example GitHub Share feedback

Why I built it

Recently, as I have been preparing my PhD applications and looking back over my research, I have noticed how much a research process leaves behind: papers, half-formed ideas, analysis notes, feedback, unfinished experiments, and decisions made in conversation.

When I return to those materials, the hardest part is often recovering what I was thinking at the time:

  • Why did this idea excite me?
  • Why did I put it aside?
  • Was I missing data or a method, or had I not worked out how to test it?
  • Could something I have learned since make it possible now?

I also discuss research with AI often. A single conversation can produce several directions worth considering. Even when I save them, they can gradually disappear into an old chat.

So I built a small tool called Research Atlas, to give these ideas a place I can keep coming back to. It connects questions, papers, ideas, methods, projects, and reflections, while keeping the reasons behind those connections and the changes in my judgment.

Imagine putting a question aside because you cannot measure a particular process. A few months later, you learn a new method. Returning to that record lets you ask something specific: could this method address the original bottleneck? What would be worth testing first?

The thinking behind it

In designing the tool with an AI assistant, I kept returning to one idea: a research workspace should preserve the thinking behind the work, not just its outputs. Three principles helped shape it.

Keep the bottleneck with the idea

"Needs more work" tells me very little months later. "Our current measurement cannot distinguish these two explanations" gives me something concrete to reconsider. A paused idea can remain useful if I remember what stopped it and what would make it worth revisiting.

Let new knowledge meet old questions

A course, a paper, a conversation, a new dataset, or a collaborator can change what is possible. I want the map to help bring those experiences into contact with questions I already care about. A useful connection explains which obstacle might now be addressed, rather than simply noting that two things seem similar.

Remember why your judgment changed

Why did I choose a small pilot over a larger study? What feedback made me revise a question? What result persuaded me to pause? Keeping those reasons makes it possible to see how my interests, capabilities, and sense of a worthwhile research question develop.

I also want it to leave room for unfinished thinking. A long-term question may lead to several different ideas; an idea can be tested with a small attempt before it becomes a project. Recording an interest should not turn it into an obligation. Learning and a well-reasoned pause can count as progress too.

Something worth returning to

Whether you are preparing to apply for a PhD, just starting one, or already juggling many projects and ideas, I hope Research Atlas can help you gradually build your own research map.

Two months later, you should be able to reopen an old idea, remember why it interested you, see what has changed, and decide on a concrete next step.

Research Atlas is open source and free to use. Your workspace is saved in your own browser, with no account required. There is a complete fictional example to explore before you start your own. Browser storage is not a backup, so export a copy of work you want to keep.

I would really like to hear how it works for you. How do you keep track of your research thinking now? What is easiest to forget? What would make this a tool you keep using? Share your experience on GitHub or email me.
