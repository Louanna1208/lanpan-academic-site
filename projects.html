Hypothesis-literature matching

Our hypothesis-literature matching work asks whether a proposed hypothesis has already been tested, even when the relevant studies use different terminology. We represent both the query and claims extracted from paper abstracts in terms of their predictor and outcome constructs, the relationship between them, and any stated conditions. This makes it possible to distinguish papers that discuss related concepts from those that support or contradict the specific claim.

In an evaluation using 107 expert-extracted hypothesis-paper pairs and 1,605 synthetic unsupported hypotheses, construct-level matching correctly rejected 87–90% of the unsupported hypotheses, compared with 31–40% when asking an LLM directly. The contribution is a testable way to compare claims beyond wording, while separating conceptual overlap from evidence for a particular relationship. It provides a first step toward comparing evidence across differences in terminology.

Pan, L., & Musslick, S. (2026). Has someone already tested this? Construct-level hypothesis-literature matching. Discovery Science 2026 (accepted).

Aligning scientific representations

LLM exploration

Our published exploration study provides a methodological foundation: model behavior can be measured, and internal representations can be analyzed and perturbed. This makes language models a tool for investigating cognitive processes, beyond evaluating how well they perform a task. We compared language models with 29,493 human players in an open-ended discovery game, examining two strategies: seeking uncertain outcomes and choosing actions that expand future possibilities, known as empowerment. Humans used both, whereas several non-reasoning models relied more heavily on uncertainty and made less use of empowerment. Reasoning models performed much better, showing that these limitations were not shared by all language models.

Sparse autoencoders identified internal features associated with these theory-defined quantities. Ablating an empowerment-related feature selectively reduced empowerment-guided behavior, linking the representation to the model's strategy through intervention. Together, these analyses connect behavioral patterns, cognitive theory, and internal computation. They also distinguish representing a strategy from using it effectively in a choice. This motivates studying task relationships through both behavior and model representations, while leaving open which findings generalize to human cognition.

Pan, L.*, Xie, H.*, & Wilson, R. C. (2025). Large language models think too fast to explore effectively. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, 38, 102480-102508. [DOI] [arXiv]

Measuring relationships between tasks

Think-aloud model discovery

Our work on automated cognitive model discovery asks whether people's verbal accounts can help distinguish explanations that their choices alone leave unresolved. Using risky decisions from 72 participants, we compared LLM-generated cognitive models discovered from choices alone with models discovered from choices and think-aloud reports together. Adding language improved model fit on held-out decisions at the group level.

Crucially, language changed the computational structures of the discovered models, not just their fit: 69.4% of participants' best-fitting models moved to a different structural family. A common shift was from explicitly comparing option values to first transforming and integrating information within each option. These results show that verbal reports can constrain which explanations model discovery selects, beyond what behavior alone provides. This offers a basis for investigating how subjective interpretations shape decisions, without assuming that a better fit by itself establishes the underlying human mechanism.

Xie, H., Jagadish, A. K., Pan, L., & Wilson, R. C. (2026). Think-aloud reshapes automated cognitive model discovery beyond behavior [Conference presentation]. 9th Annual Conference on Cognitive Computational Neuroscience (CCN). [arXiv]

Behavioral-Computational Task Space

I use LLM fine-tuning as a measurement tool: within a common model backbone, an adapter is trained on human behavior from one task and evaluated on whether it improves prediction of human behavior in another. Our current analyses find structured transfer patterns that are not well explained by task labels or semantic similarity. This provides an operational measure of task relationships, not direct evidence that people transfer learning in the same way.

Experimental representations

I am extending construct-level comparison to experimental factors and task structure through event-state representations, with cognitive control as an initial domain. The aim is to compare what experiments actually manipulate and measure, rather than rely on paradigm names, and identify changes that would test a theory's proposed scope.

PSYCHE

PSYCHE is a collaborative effort to turn dispersed behavioral datasets into a living evidence base that can be updated, checked against its sources, and reused. Working with Akshay K. Jagadish and Younes Strittmatter on a database developed by collaborators, I built the analysis agent and conduct cross-experiment analyses. The agent helps researchers identify relevant datasets, review an analysis plan, and examine results with their sources. This supports asking a common question across many experiments, including questions the original studies were not designed to address.

One current example asks: people often slow down after making a mistake, but does slowing down mean they are correcting themselves? Slower responses could reflect greater caution, but also surprise or difficulty returning attention to the task. We distinguish the magnitude of this response from its functional consequence: whether subsequent decisions become more accurate.

Our current cross-experiment analyses find slowing on average, with substantial differences across studies. Rarer errors and clearer feedback are associated with greater slowing, and these features help predict its magnitude in studies left out when fitting the model. Yet many experiments show slower but less accurate responses after errors. Across studies, the size of the slowing does not reliably indicate whether accuracy improves.

These findings question the use of slowing alone as evidence of successful error correction. They do not rule out adaptive adjustments that are outweighed by other costs. The next question is what separates slowing that accompanies improved performance from slowing that does not. For this case, an explanation should predict both response time and accuracy.
